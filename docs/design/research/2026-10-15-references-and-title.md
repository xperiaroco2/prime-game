## Part 1. How games put references in, and what makes them land

### 1.1 The homage prop: the bonfire case

**How it works.** The Dark Souls bonfire (a sword stuck in a heap of ash, burning) is the clearest example of a prop that other studios rebuild as a nod. Wikipedia's article lists bonfire easter eggs in Borderlands 2, Just Cause 3, The Witcher 3, Fallout 4, Overwatch and Days Gone, and cites critics who call the bonfire "one of the most well-received and copied ideas" from the series [V] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonfire_(Dark_Souls))). Some homages also work in play. In Assassin's Creed Valhalla you can kneel at the bonfire to restore energy ([Den of Geek](https://www.denofgeek.com/games/assassins-creed-valhalla-best-easter-eggs-guide/)). In The Witcher 3: Blood and Wine, lighting a hidden bonfire past a boss gives a relic weapon ([Twinfinite](https://twinfinite.net/news/the-witcher-3-dark-souls-easter-egg/)) [V, search summary]. Overwatch's Eichenwalde map hid one in a castle catacomb. Players on the public test server found it within days of the map going up there in August 2016, and it does nothing in play ([Gamereactor](https://www.gamereactor.eu/?p=33598), [Critical Hits](https://criticalhits.com.br/games/novo-mapa-de-overwatch-tem-easter-egg-de-dark-souls/)) [V, search summary]. The Wikipedia article mentions no lawsuit or licence dispute over any of these homages [V] (same page).

**Why it works.** [A] The prop is made of generic things (a sword, fire, ash), so each studio models its own. It uses no name, logo or texture from the original, but fans recognise the arrangement at once. The recognition is the reward, and a small gameplay bonus (healing, a weapon) makes the find worth telling friends about.

**What goes wrong.** [A] Almost nothing legally, as far as I found. The cost is that the joke is borrowed: it works only for people who know the source.

**For us.** [A] This is the safest kind of reference: an arrangement of generic objects that evokes a famous scene, built entirely from our own assets and given no name. The plasticine style helps, because every homage is redrawn by default.

### 1.2 Parody names and altered brands

- **Grand Theft Auto** has used made-up brands that echo real ones since Vice City. Its rival soft drinks are commonly read as cola parodies, and a fashion label imitates a luxury monogram. A search summary attributes to Take-Two's CEO the view that GTA VI avoids real brand placement because its world is fictional, which keeps it "clean" ([VPEsports](https://vpesports.com/games/gta/gta-6-wont-feature-real-brands-heres-why), fan wikis) [V, search summary; the quote is unverified].
- **Content Warning** (2024, proximity voice) puts its whole economy on a parody platform. The Steam description reads: "Film your friends doing scary things to become SpöökTube famous!" [V] (Steam appdetails API, app 2881650). The parody is one altered name plus a fictional business model, with no logo or interface copied [A].
- **Borderlands 2** names gear and quests after other works, for example an assault rifle named after a Willy Wonka character and a quest that replays the climax of The Lord of the Rings ([Complex](https://www.complex.com/pop-culture/a/hanuman-welch/borderlands-2-drops-a-dark-souls-easter-egg), Filibuster Blog) [V, search summary]. In 2014 Guinness World Records listed it as the video game with the most pop culture references: 411, counted on the fan wiki [V] ([Guinness](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/116153-most-pop-culture-references-in-a-videogame)).

### 1.3 Quotes as achievement and item names

This is the cheapest place to put a reference. Broken Arrow has 60+ achievements, some named after famous film lines ([FandomWire](https://fandomwire.com/this-clever-touch-in-broken-arrow-is-earning-unexpected-praise/)). FTL names achievements after Star Trek and The Dark Knight ([VGFacts](https://www.vgfacts.com/game/ftlfasterthanlight)). Ghostbusters: The Video Game named every achievement after a line from the film, and Chivalry 2 quotes Monty Python and 300 (search summary aggregating [Keengamer](https://www.keengamer.com/articles/guides/all-chivalry-2-trophy-references-to-popular-films-and-tv/) and others) [V, search summary]. [A] An achievement name costs nothing to make and can be renamed in a patch, which matters legally (Part 2.6).

### 1.4 Recreated scenes and whole parody levels

Goat Simulator 3 is the most extreme case. Reviewers list a portal that recreates a well-known Counter-Strike map, a parody of the P.T. horror corridor, a basement in 1990s graphics and a mini-game that parodies Splatoon. A search summary attributes to the creative director: "anything that happens in the world, we see if this is something that we can spoof" ([Gaming.net review](https://www.gaming.net/reviews/goat-simulator-3-review-xbox-series-x-s-ps5-pc/), [Dot Esports](https://dotesports.com/general/news/goat-simulator-3-is-chock-full-of-pop-culture-references-according-to-devs)) [V, search summary; the Dot Esports page returned 403]. The first Goat Simulator's title itself mocked the simulator genre, even imitating the font of a farming sim [V, search summary] (Tropedia). [A] Rebuilding a famous level layout is more exposed than a single prop, because a layout carries a lot of creative expression. Note that this series parodies genres and famous moments; it does not borrow characters.

### 1.5 References in the 2023-2026 proximity-voice wave (checked live)

- **Lethal Company:** its best-known nod is a randomly spawning yellow room styled after the "Backrooms" internet horror meme ([Dot Esports](https://dotesports.com/indies/news/all-easter-eggs-and-secrets-in-lethal-company)). Players then built a myth around it, arguing on Steam about whether it marks a monster's lair. Others replied that it spawns from the layout alone ([Steam thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1966720/discussions/0/4032472803480552480), [Twinfinite](https://twinfinite.net/features/all-secrets-easter-eggs-in-lethal-company/)) [V, search summary]. [A] A reference placed at random became a rumour machine, which is the "randomness is fun" pillar working through a reference.
- **Content Warning:** the parody platform (1.2).
- **PEAK:** its secrets are its own inventions, such as an in-world airline mascot that is also a findable plush (later sold as merchandise) and joke badges ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak_(video_game))) [V, search summary].
- **Mage Arena** (2025, spells spoken into the microphone): the press compares it to Harry Potter, but the sources I found show no developer statement calling it a parody ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mage_Arena), [Notebookcheck](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Shout-spells-into-your-mic-to-defeat-opponents-in-this-new-2-99-PvP-Steam-game-with-92-positive-rating-from-2-600-reviews-in-only-5-days.1071875.0.html)) [V, search summary].
- **R.E.P.O.:** I found no documented references to outside works. Searches only turned up its name's acronym and comparisons to Lethal Company [V, search summary] ([Deltia's Gaming](https://deltiasgaming.com/?p=220555)).
- [A] **Pattern:** the hits of this wave are not dense with references. Their comedy comes from their systems (round 1, B4). Their nods go to internet commons (the Backrooms meme) or to platform archetypes (a video site), not to another company's characters. This is partly absence of evidence: guides document secrets poorly.

### 1.6 What makes a reference land

- **Recognition plus exclusivity.** Media scholars at the University of Skövde (2025 interview) say easter eggs "reward a select group of fans who recognise obscure references, offering a sense of exclusivity" ([HIS](https://www.his.se/en/news/2025/april/easter-eggs--the-hidden-gems-of-film-and-games)) [P, search summary]. A survey of 200 Witcher 3 players found five player types that engage with its references very differently ([Replay journal](https://czasopisma.uni.lodz.pl/Replay/article/view/26733)) [V, search summary; abstract only].
- **Discovery is social.** The founding easter egg, Warren Robinett's hidden credit in Atari's Adventure (1979/80), was a protest against Atari's no-credit policy. Atari kept it because secrets made players keep exploring ([Atari blog](https://atari.com/blogs/atari/so-let-s-talk-about-that-easter-egg)) [V, search summary]. The Overwatch bonfire was found and shared within days (1.1).
- **Surprise and a small reward.** [A] The homages people talk about most do something: they heal, give an item or start a rumour.
- **They go stale.** Borderlands 3 was widely criticised for dated meme humour. Before Borderlands 4, its narrative director said he would "cry real tears" if "skibidi" shipped under his watch, and the series would cut back ([VGC](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/borderlands-4-will-cut-back-on-the-toilet-humour-and-meme-references-says-narrative-director), [Gfinity](https://www.gfinityesports.com/article/expect-less-meme-references-in-borderlands-4)) [V, search summary, P]. [A] References to fresh memes age within months. References to durable icons (a save-point fire, a classic film line) last.
- **Borrowing a design without credit draws backlash, even when it is legal.** When Fortnite launched its Impostors mode (2021), Innersloth said "it would've been really, really cool to collab". They argued mechanics "shouldn't be gatekept" but wished Epic had used different themes or terms. Epic later wrote "inspired by Among Us from Innersloth" in patch notes ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/uk/among-us-dev-wishes-they-could-have-worked-with-epic-on-fortnites-impostors-mode/), [Windows Central](https://windowscentral.com/epic-games-credits-among-us-dev-fortnite-mode-inspiration)) [V, search summary]. [A] Players feel the difference between a wink and a lift, and indie studios watch it closely.

## Part 2. The legal line

### 2.1 Copyright vs trademark (vs patents)

- **Copyright** protects expression: art, models, textures, music, text, code, characters, level layouts. It does not protect ideas, rules or methods. The Ukrainian statute says so outright: protection "covers only the form of expression" and "does not extend to... ideas, theories, principles, methods, procedures, processes, systems" (Law 2811-IX, Art. 7(1), 7(3)). The same article protects an "original character" and an "original title" of a work as works in their own right (Art. 7(2)) [V] ([zakon.rada.gov.ua](https://zakon.rada.gov.ua/laws/show/2811-20/print), downloaded and searched).
- **Trademark** protects names, logos and trade dress (distinctive packaging or look) used to identify goods. The test is whether consumers are likely to be confused. A trademark is registered in classes: Class 9 covers downloadable software and games, Class 41 covers entertainment and online games, Class 28 covers toys and physical games.
- **Patents** can cover mechanics. The Palworld case is a patent case, not a copyright case (2.5).
- [A] For us: a homage prop is mostly a copyright question (do not copy expression). A parody name is mostly a trademark question (do not confuse buyers). The title is purely a trademark question.

### 2.2 United States

- **Parody under fair use.** In *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose* (1994) the Supreme Court held that a commercial parody can be fair use. A parody may take what it needs to "conjure up" the original, and the "heart" of the original is "what most readily conjures up the song for parody" [V] ([Cornell LII syllabus](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/92-1292)). The opinion also separates parody, which comments on the original, from satire, which borrows a work to comment on something else and gets less room. I did not verify that passage myself: the page I fetched holds only the syllabus. [A] Most game "references" are homage, not commentary on the source, so they cannot lean on the parody defence. Their safety comes from copying no expression at all.
- **Trademarks inside expressive works.** Under the *Rogers v. Grimaldi* test, a mark used in an artistic work is actionable only if it has no artistic relevance or explicitly misleads. In *E.S.S. v. Rock Star Videos* (9th Cir. 2008), GTA San Andreas' "Pig Pen" strip club, modelled on a real club, was protected as part of a parody of Los Angeles ([Harvard JOLT](https://jolt.law.harvard.edu/digest/ess-entmt-2000-inc-v-rock-star-videos-inc)). In *AM General v. Activision* (S.D.N.Y. 2020), Humvees in Call of Duty were protected as realism ([Finnegan](https://www.finnegan.com/en/insights/blogs/incontestable/in-legal-warfare-over-humvee-trademarks-the-first-amendment-goes-beyond-the-call-of-duty-in-dismissing-am-generals-claims.html)) [V, search summary].
- **The 2023 limit.** In *Jack Daniel's v. VIP Products* (June 8, 2023, unanimous), the Supreme Court held that Rogers does not apply when a parody is used as the defendant's own brand ("Bad Spaniels" on a dog toy). Ordinary likelihood-of-confusion analysis applies instead, and parody used as a brand is not automatically shielded from dilution ([Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2023/06/supreme-court-sharply-limits-applicability)) [V, search summary]. [A] This is the key line for us. A parody name inside the world (a prop, a joke brand on a fridge) is the strong case. A parody in our title, logo, store capsule or merchandise is the weak case.
- **Real people.** Lindsay Lohan sued Take-Two over a GTA V character. New York's highest court ruled 6-0 in 2018 that the avatar was a generic, "satirical representation" not recognisable as her. The suit still ran four years ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/disciplines/court-rejects-lindsay-lohan-s-appeal-in-i-gta-v-i-likeness-lawsuit)) [V, search summary].
- **Short creative bits are not automatically free.** In *Hanagami v. Epic* (9th Cir. 2023), a choreographer's claim over a few seconds of dance in a Fortnite emote was revived: shortness alone does not defeat copyright. It settled in February 2024 ([Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2023/11/hanagami-v-epic-games), [Justia News](https://news.justia.com/choreographer-settles-copyright-lawsuit-concerning-fortnite-dance-emote)) [V, search summary]. [A] Recognisable emotes, dances and jingles are expression, not ideas.
- **Clones.** In *Tetris v. Xio* (D.N.J. 2012), a game that copied Tetris' visual expression (piece look, board size, behaviour) infringed, even though rules are unprotected ([FBA IP Insight PDF](https://www.fedbar.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/10/ipinsight-octnov2012-pdf-1.pdf)) [V, search summary].

### 2.3 European Union

- **Optional, then mandatory.** The InfoSoc Directive (2001/29, Art. 5(3)(k)) let member states allow "caricature, parody or pastiche". The 2019 DSM Directive (2019/790, Art. 17(7)) made the exception mandatory for user uploads to content-sharing platforms ([Kluwer Copyright Blog](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/copyright-blog/the-pastiche-in-copyright-law-towards-a-european-right-to-remix/)) [V, search summary]. Germany added § 51a UrhG (caricature, parody, pastiche) in force from 7 June 2021 ([gesetze-im-internet.de](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/urhg/__51a.html)) [V, search summary].
- **What parody means in the EU.** *Deckmyn* (C-201/13, 2014): a parody must evoke an existing work while being noticeably different from it, and must express humour or mockery. The rights holder still has a legitimate interest in not having its work tied to a discriminatory message ([European Law Blog](https://europeanlawblog.eu/2014/09/25/no-laughing-matter-the-right-to-parody-in-eu-copyright-law-case-deckmyn-v-vandersteen-c-20113/)) [V, search summary].
- **Pastiche (new, 2026).** *Pelham II* (C-590/23, Grand Chamber, 14 April 2026) is the first binding reading of "pastiche". It requires an objectively recognisable purpose of entering "into a dialogue with an earlier work". It is judged by a person "familiar with that existing work", and the maker's intention is irrelevant. It needs a noticeable difference from the original. It does not cover "concealed imitations... or plagiarism" ([IPKat](https://ipkitten.blogspot.com/2026/04/cjeu-delivers-pastiche-style-judgment.html)) [V]. [A] An open, recognisable, changed homage fits that test. A hidden copy does not.
- **UK** (s. 30A CDPA, since 2014): the exception applies only to "fair dealing". The IPO's non-binding guidance says using an entire song under a parody video would not qualify ([IPO guidance PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/448273/Exceptions_to_copyright_-_Guidance_for_consumers.pdf)) [V, search summary].
- **Trademark parody in the EU.** I found no source for a general parody defence in EU trademark law. That needs a lawyer.

### 2.4 Ukraine

- Law No. 2811-IX on Copyright and Related Rights (in force 1 January 2023), Art. 22(2)(9), allows using lawfully published works to create a potpourri, a "work in the genre of parody" or a "work in the genre of caricature". This is allowed "without the permission of the rights holders and free of charge, **but with the name of the author and the source of the borrowing indicated**" (Art. 22(2), opening words). Parody is defined as a work "of a comic, satirical character" towards events, persons or a lawfully published work, which "evokes associations" with them. Caricature explicitly may rework "a character of a work or the name of a character". Art. 22(4) adds the three-step test: no harm to normal exploitation, no unjustified harm to the rights holder [V] ([zakon.rada.gov.ua](https://zakon.rada.gov.ua/laws/show/2811-20/print), my translation from the Ukrainian). I found no "pastiche" in the text [V] (grep of the downloaded text).
- A 2025 practitioner guide says Ukraine has no general fair use, and that courts read parody and quotation narrowly and want a clear link to purpose and proportionate use ([Ukrainian Law Firms](https://ukrainianlawfirms.com/reviews/copyright/)) [V, search summary].
- [A] The attribution requirement stands out. If Ukrainian law applies to us, a parody of a specific work should probably name its source somewhere, for example in the credits. Whether the statute applies to a game sold worldwide on Steam, and whether a credits line satisfies it, is a question for a lawyer.

### 2.6 Valve's Steam rules and process

- Steamworks onboarding lists as not allowed: "Content you don't own or have adequate rights to" and "Content that violates the laws of any jurisdiction in which it will be available" [V] ([Steamworks onboarding](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/gettingstarted/onboarding)). The content-rules page I fetched says nothing about IP [V] ([content rules](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/gettingstarted/contentrules)).
- Steam's legal page sends copyright claims to a DMCA form and gives no trademark complaint process [V] ([Steam legal](https://store.steampowered.com/legal/)). Under US law, after a counter-notice the platform restores content "not less than 10, nor more than 14, business days" later unless the claimant sues [V] ([17 U.S.C. § 512(g)(2)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/512)).
- [A] In practice Steam removes first and resolves later (Allumeria, No Players Online, Misery, Dark and Darker). Even a baseless claim can cost a launch window. **Being able to swap a reference quickly** (rename, swap a prop by data) is our real safety valve.
- **Music and streams.** CD Projekt Red shipped a mode in Cyberpunk 2077 that swaps out a few licensed tracks, because its licence did not cover streamers ([Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2020/11/20/cyberpunk-2077-music-youtube-twitch-dmca-streamer-mode/)) [V, search summary]. [A] Our genre spreads through clips (round 1, B2). A recognisable melody, even hummed by a character, risks claims that mute or remove the clips that market the game.

### 2.7 What indie developers report

Forum advice follows one line: recreate rather than rip assets. Keep nods small. Money earned from a reference matters legally. Ask the other developer when a nod is substantial ("a casual go-ahead" for small things, writing for bigger ones). If you have to ask whether something is safe, avoid it or ask a lawyer ([Unity Discussions](https://discussions.unity.com/t/super-mario-clone-as-an-easter-egg-is-it-legal/682120), Polycount, itch.io threads) [P, search summary]. These are not legal sources.

### 2.8 A risk ladder (summary)

| Lower risk | | | Higher risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genre tropes, generic ideas (a haunted basement, a save-point fire) | Homage prop from own assets, no name | Altered parody names of brands or games inside the world; short quotes as achievement names | Another work's names, logos, characters, music, voices, models, distinctive trade dress; real people; parody in our title, logo, store page or merchandise; a famous dance or jingle |

[A] Each step to the right needs more care. The far-right column needs a licence or a lawyer.

### 3.1 What I searched and found

- **Steam** (store search API, US): "My Alien Prime Friends", "Alien Prime", "Prime Friends", "My Alien Friends" and "Party Aliens" give 0 results. "Alien Friends" gives 1 result: *Alien Friends* by FromDown Games, "coming soon", an action game about landing on Earth and eating pixel people [V] (API query; [appdetails 5341720](https://store.steampowered.com/app/5341720/)). ### 3.2 How risky "Prime" looks [A]

- **For it:** "prime" is an ordinary English word. The field is crowded, with hundreds of live marks and many games carrying it alongside Amazon, so no owner can claim the word alone. Our full phrase matches no mark or game. A long title made of several words is less likely to be confused with a single-word mark.
- **Against it:** Amazon's PRIME now covers online video games, and Amazon's brand is famous, which adds a dilution argument in the US. The Monster Energy, Saga and Scrolls cases show that owners of common words do oppose game titles. The defendant usually won or settled, but each fight cost time, and Ubisoft simply renamed. The risk mostly bites at registration (an opposition) or at a store-page launch, not during development.
- **Search crowding** is a certain cost: Google Play already ranks Prime Video first for our exact title.
- **Other words:** "My ... Friends" titles exist (My Talking Tom Friends by Outfit7; its trademark status is not confirmed ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/1473424857))) but share only generic words. ALIEN and FRIENDS are franchise marks, but only as single words; an ordinary use in a phrase is a weak claim. Only a lawyer's clearance can confirm this.
- **Could not search:** EUIPO eSearch (JavaScript page, returned no results), TMview (API gave no response), WIPO Global Brand Database (not attempted; JavaScript), Ukraine's IP office (not attempted), console stores, the Epic Games Store, Google Play beyond its first page, and common-law (unregistered) uses. A professional clearance search covers these; it costs money, so it is the engineer's call.

## (1) Cross-game patterns

1. **Rebuild, never rip.** Every homage that stays clean is redrawn from generic parts with no name (bonfires), or uses an altered name (SpöökTube, GTA brands). The cases that went wrong involved ripped assets, look-alike characters, title words or patents.
2. **Cheap surfaces carry most references:** achievement names, item names, posters, loading tips. They cost little and can be renamed in a patch.
3. **The best references do something:** heal, give an item, start a rumour. A random or hidden nod creates its own discovery threads (Overwatch PTR, Lethal Company's room).
4. **Density divides players.** 411 references made Borderlands 2 a record holder, but its meme humour later dated badly enough that the studio cut back. Durable icons age better than fresh memes.
5. **The proximity-voice hits use few IP references.** Their comedy is systemic. Their nods go to internet commons and platform archetypes.
6. **Courtesy matters to peers.** Copying a peer's design without credit draws public backlash even when it is legal (Innersloth), and players can punish a claimant who looks like a bully (Schedule I).
7. **Platforms remove first.** On Steam, even a false notice costs days or weeks.

## (2) Risks for our design

- **The title.** "Prime" sits in Amazon's registered game services and buries our search results. Changing a title after a store page, trailers and wishlists exist is expensive [A].
- **Marketing is the weak spot.** A parody in the title, logo, capsule art or merchandise loses the in-world Rogers protection (Jack Daniel's) [A].
- **Music, dances and voice lines.** These are short but protected (Hanagami), and they threaten the clips the game spreads through [A].
- **Real people.** Cringe comedy invites caricatures of celebrities or politicians. Lohan lost, but only after four years in court [A].
- **Style drift.** The plasticine look could drift towards a famous stop-motion studio's specific characters. A style is generally free; specific characters are not [A]. A lawyer should confirm this if it ever comes up.
- **Ageing and audience split.** Teen and adult audiences recognise different things, and meme references date within months (Borderlands 3) [A].
- **The pillars.** A reference that hides information or needs outside knowledge to win would break "open knowledge". A reference that pays points becomes something to exploit, which the engineer already flagged for sabotage points [A].
- **Mods later.** If we ever open a workshop, users will upload other companies' characters, and we would inherit a takedown like Garry's Mod's [A].
- **Jurisdiction.** If Ukrainian law applies, its parody exception requires attribution, which we would have to plan for [A].

## (3) Options for the engineer (not decisions; all values are placeholders)

**Option A: an "own-made allusion only" content rule.** Every reference is built from our own assets, unnamed or under an altered name, with no outside logos, characters, music, voices or real people, and only inside the game world, never in the title, store art or merchandise.
*For:* matches every clean case found and needs no lawyer for routine content. *Against:* rules out exact quotes and recognisable sounds, so some jokes get weaker. *Cost:* a short doc page.

**Option B: an in-fiction parody frame, "the aliens learned Earth from bad broadcasts".** Every Earth reference passes through the aliens' misunderstanding: props, names and party rituals are recognisable but wrong. This is an original framing for our game, though the "alien misreads Earth" idea is a generic trope.
*For:* it builds in what *Deckmyn* and *Pelham II* ask for (evokes the original, is noticeably different, is humorous), fits the cringe tone and gives the writers a voice. *Against:* every reference has to be "bent", which takes more writing effort. Humans in disguise also need their own comic angle.

**Option C: references as random lucky breaks.** Rare variants of props, sounds or lines appear with a placeholder chance (for example 1 in 20 matches per map). Using one triggers a harmless funny effect (a pose, a sting, confetti) and never points, mutations or information.
*For:* serves "randomness is part of the fun", produces clips and rumours (as Lethal Company's room did), and keeps points safe from exploits and open knowledge intact. *Against:* rarity means many players never see one. It needs tuning, and the effects must stay cosmetic.

**Option D: a reference ledger in content data.** Each reference prop, name or line is a `Resource` with fields such as `homage_of` (free text), `risk_tier` (green/amber/red; the tiers are placeholders) and `swap_to` (a neutral fallback). A check fails on red. One switch can swap any entry within hours of a notice.
*For:* fits "mechanics are data", turns Steam's remove-first process into a quick fix, and gives Ukrainian-style attribution a source list for the credits. *Against:* tooling work, and it adds an engine change to the content API. Adding a field to a content resource also changes the engine–content contract, which needs the engineer's word.

**Option E: licensed nods with peer indies, later.** Exchange small, written cross-references with other friendslop studios.
*For:* the Innersloth lesson turned around: a goodwill story for marketing. *Against:* needs contracts and relationships, probably a lawyer, and it is not useful before release.

**Option F: the title.** Three paths:
- (F1) keep "My Alien Prime Friends" as a working title only, and decide before any public Steam page after a paid clearance search covering the US, EU, UK and Ukraine;
- (F2) replace "Prime" with an invented word that we can own and that ranks in search;
- (F3) keep "Prime" with a distinctive logo and no Amazon-like colours, accepting the search crowding.

*Trade-offs:* F1 costs money and delays the decision. F2 costs the joke the engineer likes and needs a new name. F3 is cheapest now and riskiest later.

**Recommendation:** A + B as the content rule, C for the fun, and D when content volume grows. For the title, F1 now, with F2 as the fallback if the clearance or the engineer's reason for "Prime" (open question 1) does not hold up. All of this is for the engineer to decide.

## (4) Open questions for the engineer

1. What does "Prime" mean in the title: the project name, an in-joke, prime numbers, "in their prime"? Would the joke survive with a different word?
2. Where will the game be published from, and under whose name (a person or a company, which country)? This decides which law applies, including Ukraine's attribution rule.
3. Is there a budget for one paid trademark clearance and one hour with an IP lawyer before a public Steam page? Both cost money, so both are your call.
4. Tone limits: may the game parody real brands, real people (celebrities, politicians), other games' famous scenes? Which are off limits?
5. May references ever change gameplay (a heal, an item), or must they stay cosmetic so points and open knowledge stay clean?
6. Density: roughly how many references per map, and should they lean on durable icons or on current memes?
7. Should we ask other developers before nodding to their games (the Innersloth lesson), or only for big nods?
8. Will the game ever accept user content (maps, skins)? If yes, a takedown policy is needed before that ships.
